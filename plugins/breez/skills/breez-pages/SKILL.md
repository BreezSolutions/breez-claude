# Designing a page people use

A page is for someone who opens it cold, with a question and a few seconds. Design it the way the best internal tools are designed: it answers first, it is organized the way its reader works, and every figure on it can be trusted at a glance and checked in one click.

## Fit the page to how it is used
- A page someone works from day to day — many moving parts, several people, deadlines — is a tool: a few views, each for a question people ask of it, and every open item with who has it, who it waits on and when it is due.
- A page someone checks now and then — one invoice, a settled account — is a short page that fits on a screen. Navigation there only hides things.
- Make a page no bigger than its job: every view is one someone would open. Each extra part is one more thing to keep true.

## Answer first
- The top of the page is the answer: where it stands, what needs attention, by when, and the few figures that decide it. Someone who reads only that is not misled.
- Lead with what someone has to do, soonest first, with the action itself beside it. What is fine can be quiet.
- Say what is late, at risk or blocked in words and in color together; color alone is missed.

## Organize the way the reader works
- Group by the reader's units of work and by time, not by where the information came from or by record type.
- When a page is more than a screen, give it navigation: a few named views (tabs or a side menu) instead of one long scroll, each answering one question. Keep the views and their order the same on every page of its kind, so a reader who knows one knows all.
- Show the summary; let the detail open on demand (a row that expands, a view one click away). Never make someone read detail to find the summary.
- Lists that grow get filters and a sort that matches the question (what's open, what's mine, what's waiting on others, what's late).
- An address that someone can send should open the same view: links to a section or tab land there.

## Make every figure trustworthy
- Every figure and every claim carries its source, cited where it sits — in the table cell, beside the number, inside the sentence — not in a list at the end. A reader should never wonder where a number came from.
- Cite the source's own words, the passage that proves it; a number cites the line that states it.
- Show how fresh it is: the day its sources were read, and for anything that changes fast, when it was last checked.
- Say what you don't know as plainly as what you do. An unknown is shown as unknown, with what would settle it — never a guess styled as a fact, never a blank that looks like zero.
- A claim that something is missing or hasn't happened says where you looked.
- Figures that should agree must agree: a total equals its lines, a summary equals its detail. If two sources disagree, show both and say which you used.

## Numbers people compare
- Put a number beside what it should be compared to: its budget, its target, last period, the deadline. A number alone is rarely the answer.
- Right-align figures with the same precision and units; give tables their totals and subtotals.
- Use a bar, a mark on a bar or a small chart when the comparison matters more than the digits; keep the digits beside it.

## Actions in place
- Each thing to do sits where it is explained, with the action that does it. Show each action once, in full; elsewhere point to it compactly.
- Make who is waiting on whom explicit: what we owe, what others owe us, and since when.

## Restraint
- Short labels, plain words, no jargon the reader wouldn't use. Every word, color and line earns its place.
- Use the house style's components and the app's colors, consistently: one look for one meaning. No decoration, no emoji as icons, no clever layouts that make a reader learn the page.
- Dense is fine; cluttered is not. Align things, group related things, and let whitespace separate groups.
- It must read well at any width: columns wrap rather than overflow, and names never break mid-word.

## Built to stay true
- Keep the page's facts in one data block and its code apart, so bringing it up to date changes the data, not the design.
- The page must render whatever the data holds: nothing yet, one item, many, a missing value.
- Before you finish, look at it as the reader would: open each view, check that the answer is at the top, that each figure has its source, and that nothing contradicts anything else.
